Behind the model

How fast do wings beat?

The comparison uses real published observations where we found them. For birds without a species measurement, it uses a labelled estimate based on a measured reference bird.

One beat is a complete downstroke and upstroke. A rate of 4 beats per second is 240 beats per minute. A bird soaring with open wings makes zero wingbeats during that period.

The eleven comparison birds

These are approximate rates for active flapping. Wingbeat frequency changes with speed, manoeuvre, weather, body size and whether the bird is taking off, cruising or hovering.

How estimates were made

Research on steady cruising flight predicts that wingbeat frequency tends to change with body mass to the power of minus one sixth when birds have similar proportions. Our estimate starts with a published rate for a reference bird, then calculates estimated rate = reference rate × (bird mass ÷ reference mass)−1/6. The measured reference rates come from the eLife bird data table; the rule comes from Pennycuick’s flight research.

The rule is a rough comparison, especially when the reference bird has a different wing shape. The kookaburra and flamingo proxies are particularly coarse. These estimates should never be read as measured wingbeat rates for those species. The barn owl field study, golden eagle field study, and Cornell hummingbird profile provide the species-specific values.

Why the models move slowly

All comparison wings play at one eighth of the listed rate. This keeps their relative timing while making rapid beats visible on a classroom screen. The readout shows the bird’s full rate, not the slow-motion playback rate. The 3D models teach body and wing differences; they are not anatomical scans or an aerodynamic prediction.
